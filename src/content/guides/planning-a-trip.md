Planning a trip used to mean a pile of guidebooks and a dozen browser tabs. Now you can describe what you'd like — "a gentle week in Portugal in May, not too much walking, good food, one or two day trips" — and get a full outline back in a minute.

It's one of the best everyday uses of AI. It's also the one where a confident wrong answer costs real money. So here's how to get the good part without the expensive part.

## What AI is excellent at

**The shape of a trip.** "Five days in Vienna for two people in their sixties who like music and cafés" gets you a sensible day-by-day plan: which neighbourhoods, which sights are near each other, where a rest day fits. This is the tedious part of planning, and AI does it in seconds.

**Answering the questions you'd be embarrassed to ask.** "Is it rude to tip in Japan?" "Do I need an adapter for Italian sockets?" "How do you pronounce 'Bruges'?" It's patient, it doesn't judge, and it's usually right about things millions of travellers have asked before.

**Comparing options.** "Train or coach from Edinburgh to Inverness — which is more comfortable for someone with a bad hip?" It'll lay out the trade-offs clearly.

**Packing lists.** Tell it where, when, how long, and anything special (walking poles, medication, a wedding). You'll get a list to tick off.

**Drafting the awkward emails.** "Write to the hotel asking for a ground-floor room because of my knee." (See [writing a difficult email](/guides/difficult-email/).)

## What AI is bad at — and this is the important bit

**Anything with a number or a time.** Opening hours, prices, train times, whether a museum is closed on Mondays. The AI's knowledge has a cut-off date, and even before that, it's working from patterns, not a timetable. It will state "the museum opens at 9" with total confidence and be wrong. **Every time, every number: check the official website.**

**Whether something still exists.** Restaurants close. Hotels change hands. Ferry routes get cancelled. AI will happily recommend a café that shut in 2022.

**Availability and booking.** It cannot see whether a hotel has rooms on your dates. If it says "book the Hotel Bella Vista," that's a suggestion, not a reservation, and not a promise that it's any good today.

**Specific travel rules.** Visa requirements, what you can carry in hand luggage, whether your passport needs six months' validity. These change and they matter. Use the official government travel site for your country — it exists precisely for this.

## The method: plan with AI, verify with the source, book with a human-facing site

Here's the routine that keeps you safe:

**1. Sketch with AI.** Describe the trip. Get the outline. Ask follow-ups. Enjoy this part — it's fun.

**2. Ask it to list what needs checking.** Literally: "Which parts of this plan should I verify before booking?" It's good at flagging its own weak spots — it'll tell you to check the ferry times and the museum days.

**3. Check each one at the source.** The museum's own website. The railway's own timetable. Your government's travel advice page. Ten minutes, and you've turned a nice-sounding plan into a real one.

**4. Book on sites you'd have used anyway.** Your usual booking site, the airline directly, the hotel directly. Never through a link the AI gives you — links in AI answers can be out of date or, in rare cases, wrong altogether.

## A worked example

*You type:* "Plan a relaxed four-day trip to Copenhagen for two people in our late sixties, in September. We like gardens, good coffee, and we don't want to walk more than a couple of miles a day. One day trip would be nice."

*It gives you:* a day-by-day plan — Tivoli and the harbour on day one, the botanical garden and a café district on day two, a day trip to a castle by train on day three, a gentle last day. Sensible, well-grouped, and a good starting point.

*You then check:* Is Tivoli open in September? (Its website says yes, but hours vary — noted.) Which train goes to the castle, and how often? (Railway site.) Is the botanical garden's glasshouse open? (Its own page says it's closed for repairs — good thing you looked.)

*You then ask the AI:* "The glasshouse is closed. What's a similar gentle alternative for that morning?" And it adjusts.

That's the relationship: it plans, you verify, it adjusts. Nobody's paid for a closed glasshouse.

## Two small extras worth knowing

- **Ask for accessibility explicitly.** "Which of these have steps?" "Is there seating?" It'll often know, and it'll remind you to confirm.
- **Ask it to make you a checklist for the day before you leave.** Passport, chargers, medication, a printed copy of bookings. The dull stuff it's good at.

## The one thing to remember

AI is a superb planning *partner* and an unreliable *source*. Let it do the thinking; do the checking yourself, at the real website, before any money changes hands.
