Because the companies that built them chose it that way — not because a computer voice has to sound like anything in particular. When Siri, Alexa and Google's assistant were being designed, the teams behind them tested different voices and, by their own account, found that people warmed to a female-sounding voice for a helpful, service-style assistant. That preference got built in as the default. It isn't a rule of engineering. It's a decision, made by people, and it can be changed.

## It started with a choice, not a requirement

There's nothing about how these voices are made that requires them to sound female. A computer voice is generated from recordings of a real person's speech, then shaped by software into whatever tone the company wants. Early voice assistants could just as easily have defaulted to a male voice, a neutral one, or let you choose from the start. They didn't, mostly because of what the companies' own testing suggested people preferred — and because the idea of a polite, ever-available helper already had a long history of being imagined as a woman, going back to telephone switchboard operators and secretaries.

That history matters, because it's part of why the choice felt so natural to the companies making it at the time. If you've ever felt a flicker of unease that the "assistant" in your kitchen or your phone is female by default while you, the one giving instructions, are not — you're not imagining a pattern. Researchers and journalists have pointed to the same thing for years: giving assistants a female voice by default can quietly reinforce the old idea that assisting is naturally a woman's job. The companies involved have heard that criticism, which is one reason most of them now let you pick.

## You can usually change the voice

If the default doesn't sit right with you, or you simply prefer a different sound, you don't have to keep it. Here's roughly where to look:

- **On an iPhone or iPad (Siri):** open Settings, tap Siri & Search, then Siri Voice. You can choose between several voices and accents.
- **On an Amazon Echo (Alexa):** open the Alexa app, go to Settings, then Device Settings, choose your device, and look for Wake Word and Voice options. Amazon has also offered a well-known male voice as an alternative in some markets.
- **On an Android phone (Google Assistant or Gemini):** open the Google app or Settings, find Assistant settings, then Assistant Voice, and pick from the options shown.

The exact wording and number of steps can shift when these companies update their apps, so if a menu doesn't match exactly, look for "voice" under the assistant's own settings — it's usually close by. If you can't find it at all, that's worth mentioning to whoever set the device up for you rather than assuming it isn't possible.

## Chatbots are a slightly different case

Everything above is about *voice* assistants — the ones you talk to out loud. Text-based chatbots like [ChatGPT](/guides/chatgpt-explained/) don't have a voice at all unless you turn one on, and when you type to them, there's no gender attached to the words on the screen. Some of these chatbots now offer an optional spoken mode, and in that mode you're often given a small choice of voices, some sounding male and some female, rather than one fixed default. So the "always female" pattern is really a habit from the older generation of voice assistants — the ones built to sit on your counter or live in your phone and take instructions — more than something true of AI in general. ## Why the companies are changing course

Over the past few years, several of the companies behind these assistants have quietly added more voice choices, and some have made the choice a genuinely open one rather than a "default plus alternative." That shift has come from a mix of pressure — from researchers, journalists and customers who found the pattern uncomfortable — and from the practical fact that offering choice costs a company very little once the voices exist. None of this means the original decision was made maliciously; it was closer to companies following what their testing told them and not thinking hard enough about the wider message it sent. But it does mean you're entitled to pick whichever voice you find most pleasant to listen to, without worrying that you're doing something unusual.

## It's worth remembering the voice isn't the AI

Whichever voice you choose, it's worth keeping one thing separate in your mind: the voice is just the packaging. It doesn't tell you anything about how clever, honest or reliable the assistant is underneath. A confident-sounding voice, of any kind, can still give you a wrong answer just as easily as an uncertain one — which is exactly why it's worth [checking anything that matters before you rely on it](/guides/when-ai-is-wrong/), whatever the voice sounds like. ## The short version

- Most AI voices default to female because early testing suggested people responded well to it — it isn't a technical necessity.
- The pattern has older roots too, in the long-standing idea of assistants and switchboard operators as female roles.
- You can change the voice on Siri, Alexa and Google Assistant through their settings menus.
- Text chatbots like ChatGPT usually have no voice at all unless you switch one on, and often offer a real choice when you do.
- The voice tells you nothing about how accurate the assistant is — check what matters, regardless of how it sounds.
