The best way to learn AI is to stop treating it like a subject you have to study, and start treating it like a tool you try once, on one small thing. Pick a single task — writing a birthday message, working out what to cook, understanding a confusing letter — and do it with an AI chatbot. Nothing more. Once that feels ordinary, add a second task. That's the whole method. There's no course to finish, no exam, and no age by which you're supposed to already know this.

## You don't need a course, or a computer science degree

A lot of "learn AI" advice online is aimed at people who want a career in it — courses on writing code, technical certificates, hours of video lectures. None of that is what you need. Using an AI chatbot well is closer to learning to use a new phone than learning a new subject: you don't read a manual cover to cover, you press buttons and see what happens.

The three well-known chatbots — ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini — are all free to try and need nothing installed. You open a page in your web browser, type a sentence, and it writes back. If you haven't done this yet, [our step-by-step first conversation guide](/guides/your-first-conversation/) walks through it slowly, with nothing to get wrong.

## Start with one thing, not everything

The mistake people make at any age, not just later in life, is trying to learn "AI" as a whole. It isn't one thing. It's more like learning to cook: you don't learn all of cooking at once, you learn to make one dish, then another. Pick a task that's already part of your week and try it there first:

> I'm writing a card for my niece's wedding. Can you suggest a warm, short message — not too soppy?

> Here's a letter from my energy supplier. Can you explain what it's actually asking me to do, in plain English?

> I have leftover roast chicken and some vegetables. What's a simple meal I could make tonight?

Notice that none of these need any special wording or technical terms. You're just asking, the way you'd ask a helpful neighbour. If the first answer isn't quite right, say so — "shorter, please" or "that's not what I meant" both work perfectly well, and the AI will try again without getting impatient. [Ten everyday things AI can genuinely help with](/guides/everyday-uses/) is a good source of first tasks if you're not sure where to start.

## Little and often beats a weekend of trying to master it

You do not learn this in one long sitting, and trying to is what makes people give up. Ten minutes with one small task, a few times a week, teaches you more than an entire Saturday afternoon spent trying to understand everything at once. Each time, you'll notice the box, the reply, and the rhythm of typing and reading become a little more familiar — the way a new pair of glasses stops feeling strange after a few days.

If a session goes badly — a confusing answer, a page that looks different from last time — close it and try again another day. Nothing is lost by stopping. There's no progress bar to reset, no course you've fallen behind on.

## It's fine to ask the same question your grandchild already knows

One thing that puts people off learning anything new later in life is the worry that they're asking something "everyone already knows." With AI, that worry doesn't apply in the way you'd expect: it changes often enough, and unevenly enough, that plenty of confident younger users don't actually know how it works either — they've just used it more, on fewer things.

If you have a grandchild, child, or friend who already uses AI chatbots, it's worth asking them to sit with you for ten minutes rather than explaining it in writing. Watching someone type a question and get a reply, then rephrase it when the answer isn't quite right, teaches the rhythm of it faster than reading about it ever will. And you may well ask a question that gets them thinking too — "can it get things wrong?" is a fair one, and the honest answer is yes, sometimes confidently so, which is worth knowing from the start. [How to tell when AI is wrong](/guides/when-ai-is-wrong/) covers that properly.

## Learning it safely as you go

You don't need to learn "AI safety" as a separate topic before you begin — you can pick it up alongside the ordinary tasks. The one habit worth building early: don't type anything into a chatbot that you wouldn't want a stranger to read, such as full account numbers, passwords, or your National Insurance number. Beyond that, the honest answer is that most everyday questions — recipes, letters, messages, explanations — carry no real risk at all.

As you get more comfortable, you'll naturally start noticing things: which kinds of questions it answers well, which it fumbles, when it's worth double-checking an answer against something else. That noticing is the actual learning. It happens on its own, through use, not through reading a guide like this one twice.

## When you're ready for more

Once one or two tasks feel completely ordinary, you can widen out — try a different chatbot, ask it to help plan something bigger, or explore a feature like reading a document aloud to you. There's no rush and no deadline. Plenty of people use AI happily for years while only ever doing the handful of things that are genuinely useful to them, and that's a perfectly complete way to use it. You are not behind by learning it slowly. You are simply learning it the way that actually works.

## The short version

- You don't need a course or technical knowledge — just one small task to start with.
- Pick something already part of your week: a message, a letter, a recipe.
- Short, frequent tries beat one long session. Stop and come back another day if it goes badly.
- Asking someone who already uses it to show you for ten minutes helps more than reading about it.
- Never type passwords or full account numbers into a chatbot; almost everything else is low-risk.
- There's no finish line. Widen out only when you feel like it.
