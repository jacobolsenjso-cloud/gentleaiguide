It's the question people ask quietly, half-hoping the answer is yes. The doctor gives you eight minutes. The AI gives you all night, explains everything twice, and never sighs. So — can it take over?

No. But it can do something almost as valuable: make your eight minutes with the doctor count for twenty. Here's where the line is, and why.

## What AI is genuinely good at

**Explaining.** "What is a hiatus hernia, in plain English?" "What does 'elevated cholesterol' actually mean?" "My letter says 'benign' — is that good?" AI is excellent at this. It has read every patient leaflet ever written and can rephrase them for you without jargon and without hurry. This alone is worth a lot: most people leave appointments having understood half of what was said.

**Preparing you.** "I have an appointment about knee pain. What questions should I ask?" You'll get a sensible list — how long it's been going on, what makes it worse, what treatments exist, what happens if we do nothing. Walk in with that list and you'll get a better appointment.

**Helping you describe things.** People struggle to explain symptoms. "It sort of aches, but not always." Ask AI: "Help me describe this pain so the doctor understands." It'll prompt you — where, when, what kind, what helps — and you'll walk in with clear words.

**Reassurance about the ordinary.** "Is it normal to be more tired at 70 than at 50?" Yes, and AI will tell you so kindly and explain why. Not every worry needs an appointment.

**Understanding what you've been told.** [Paste in the hospital letter](/guides/explain-this-letter/) (with your name and numbers blanked out) and ask what it means. Then bring your questions to the doctor.

## What AI cannot do — and why this isn't just caution

**It cannot examine you.** It can't feel the lump, listen to your chest, see the colour of your skin, or notice that you've lost weight since last time. Half of medicine is looking. AI is blind.

**It doesn't know your history.** Your doctor knows you had that operation in 2019, that your mother had the same thing, that you're on three other medications. AI knows only what you type this minute — and [you shouldn't type all of that](/guides/never-type-this/).

**It can be confidently wrong.** This is the one that matters most. AI generates an answer that *sounds* right based on patterns. For common questions the pattern is strong and the answer is reliable. For *your specific* combination of symptoms, age, medications and history, the pattern is thin — and it will sound exactly as confident either way. ([Why this happens.](/guides/when-ai-is-wrong/)) A doctor who's unsure says "let's run a test." AI doesn't know it's unsure.

**It can't take responsibility.** If a doctor gets it wrong, there's a system — a second opinion, a complaint, a record. If AI gets it wrong, there's nothing. That's not a small difference when it's your health.

## The two questions never to outsource

**"Should I stop taking this?"** Never on AI's say-so. Medication decisions depend on things it can't see. Ask the pharmacist or doctor — both are easier to reach than people think.

**"Is this serious?"** If you're asking because you're worried it might be — chest pain, sudden weakness, confusion, bleeding that won't stop, a lump that's new — that's not a chatbot question. That's a phone call. AI will often tell you the same thing; don't wait for it to.

## The good pattern: AI before and after, doctor in the middle

**Before the appointment:** understand your symptoms, prepare your questions, learn the words.

**In the appointment:** the doctor examines, decides, prescribes. You ask your prepared questions. You take notes (or ask if you can record — many doctors say yes).

**After the appointment:** paste your notes or the letter into AI and ask "what did this mean, and what am I supposed to do now?" Then, if something doesn't make sense, ring the surgery.

People who do this report the same thing: the doctor takes them more seriously, because they arrive prepared and ask good questions. That's the real "AI in healthcare" — not a replacement, but a better patient.

## A word about the tone

AI is kind, patient and endlessly available, and at 2 a.m. that feels like care. It isn't, quite. It's a very good text machine. Enjoy the patience, use the explanations, and remember that the person who actually knows you is the one with the stethoscope.

## The short version

AI explains; the doctor examines. Use AI to understand and prepare, use the doctor to diagnose and decide, and never let a chatbot talk you out of a phone call you were worried enough to consider.
