The short answer: AI slop is low-quality writing, pictures or videos made by AI and posted in huge numbers, with little or no care about whether they are true, useful or even sensible. Think of it as the junk mail of the internet, only it arrives in your social media feed instead of your letterbox.

If you have ever seen a very odd picture of a log cabin carved from a single giant loaf of bread, a heart-warming photo of a child holding a sign that nobody can quite explain, or a long article that says a lot and tells you nothing, you have probably met it. This guide explains what slop is, why it exists and how to recognise it, without making you suspicious of everything you read.

## Where the word comes from

"Slop" is an old word for sloppy, low-grade stuff, like the scraps once thrown to farm animals. People started using it for AI-made rubbish because it fits: it is produced quickly, in bulk, with no real thought behind it.

It is not an official or technical term. Nobody measures slop with a ruler. It is simply a popular way of saying "this was churned out by a machine, and nobody cared whether it was any good".

Not everything made with AI is slop. A person who uses AI to help draft a letter, then reads it and corrects it, is not making slop. The difference is care. Slop is what you get when nobody checks, nobody minds and the only aim is to be seen. If you would like the basics first, [what AI actually is](/guides/what-is-ai/) is a good starting point.

## Why anyone makes it

The reason is almost always attention, and attention can turn into money. Many websites and social media pages earn something when people look at, like or share their posts. AI tools can make a picture or an article in seconds, so someone can post dozens a day at almost no cost.

Some of it is made for clicks. Some of it is made to push people towards a website full of adverts. Some is made to build up a page with lots of followers, which can later be used to sell things or spread other messages. A little of it is made as a joke. The common thread is quantity over quality.

That is also why it spreads. If a post makes you feel something strong, such as warmth, sympathy or irritation, you are more likely to react, and the platform shows it to more people.

## What slop looks like

Slop comes in a few common shapes. None of these signs proves anything on its own, but together they are a useful guide.

**Pictures.** Look for a scene that is too perfect, too glossy or slightly strange. Hands with odd fingers, writing that turns to gibberish, objects that melt into each other, or animals and food combined in ways that make no sense. Many slop pictures are also built to tug at your feelings: a lonely old soldier, a poor child, a miracle cake.

**Writing.** Articles that run on for a long time without saying anything specific. They repeat the same point in different words, sound confident but give no sources, and are often padded with a lot of general advice. Be wary of pages that seem to answer every question on the internet with the same flat tone.

**Videos.** Clips with an odd, smooth look, a voice that sounds a little flat, or a story that seems to come from nowhere. If it looks like a news report but you have never heard of the source, treat it with care. For fake videos in particular, [how to detect fake AI videos](/guides/detect-fake-videos/) goes through the checks one by one.

## Is slop dangerous?

Mostly it is just annoying and a waste of your time. But there are real downsides.

It can mislead you. A made-up health tip or a pretend news story can look just as convincing as a real one. It can crowd out better material, so the useful thing you were searching for is harder to find. And slop pages are sometimes used as bait: the picture is the hook and the link beneath it is the trap.

So the sensible approach is not fear. It is the same habit you would apply to any unsolicited post: pause before you believe it, and certainly before you click, share or send money.

## How to deal with it

You do not need special software. A few calm habits go a long way.

1. **Check the source.** Who posted this? Is it a page you know, or one you have never heard of that posts the same sort of thing all day? Tap the name and look.
2. **Ask whether anyone trustworthy is reporting it.** A real event will be covered by news outlets you already know. If it appears only on odd pages, be doubtful.
3. **Notice how it makes you feel.** If a post is clearly designed to make you cry, rage or rush, slow down. That pull is often the whole point.
4. **Do not share it "just in case".** Passing slop on is how it spreads. If you are not sure, leave it.
5. **Use the controls.** Most platforms let you choose "not interested", hide a post or block a page. Doing this a few times usually changes what you are shown.

If you are unsure whether something is true, ask a person you trust, or look it up on a website you already know, typed in yourself rather than reached by a link. It also helps to remember that AI can sound sure while being wrong, which is covered in [how to tell when AI is wrong](/guides/when-ai-is-wrong/).

## When to talk to someone

If a post asks you for money, a password, a bank card number or personal details, stop. Do not follow the link. If you have already clicked or paid, contact your bank using the number on your card and tell someone you trust. For anything about your health, do not rely on a post in your feed: speak to your doctor or pharmacist.

## The short version

- AI slop is careless, mass-produced AI content made to grab attention, not to help you.
- Not all AI-made material is slop. The difference is whether a person cared enough to check it.
- Odd details, vague writing and a strong emotional pull are common signs.
- Check who posted it, and whether trusted sources agree.
- When in doubt, do not share it, do not click and do not pay. Ask someone you trust.
