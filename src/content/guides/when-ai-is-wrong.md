Here is the most important sentence on this entire website:

> **AI sounds exactly as confident when it's wrong as when it's right.**

A person who isn't sure usually shows it — they hesitate, they say "I think…".
AI has no such tell. The polish of the answer tells you *nothing* about its
accuracy. Once you truly absorb that, you're safer than most daily users.

## Why does it get things wrong?

Remember how AI works: it learned patterns from enormous amounts of text, and
it answers by producing what *usually* comes next. When it doesn't know
something, it doesn't fall silent — it produces something that
*sounds* right. The industry calls this a **hallucination**. It isn't lying;
lying requires knowing the truth. It simply has no built-in sense of the
difference between remembering and inventing.

## Where it's usually reliable — and where to be careful

**Usually reliable:** explaining well-known concepts, rewriting and summarising
text you give it, everyday cooking and household questions, grammar and
translation, gift ideas, patient step-by-step help with your phone or computer.

**Double-check before acting:** anything involving your **health or
medications**, **money, taxes or the law**, **recent news**, precise **facts
about people** (AI mixes people up more than you'd think), and **phone numbers,
addresses, opening hours or prices** — it will confidently produce outdated or
invented ones.

The dividing line is simple: *does acting on a wrong answer cost me anything?*
A dull dinner suggestion costs nothing. A wrong pill interaction does.

## Four habits that catch most mistakes

1. **Ask "how sure are you?"** — it won't always know, but asking often makes it
   list the parts it is unsure about. Treat those as the bits to check.
2. **Ask where it comes from.** "What is that based on? Can you give sources?"
   Modern chatbots can often search the internet and show links. Links you can
   check are worth far more than a fluent paragraph.
3. **Get a second opinion for anything important** — from a search engine,
   an official website, or a human professional. For health questions, AI is
   excellent at *explaining* ("what does blood pressure actually measure?") and
   the wrong place for *decisions* ("should I stop taking this?").
4. **Trust your nose.** If an answer surprises you — a fact that seems too
   neat, a quote that fits too perfectly — that feeling is data. Check it.

## A five-minute exercise that will teach you a lot

Ask a chatbot about something you know deeply — your trade, your town's
history, a craft you've practised for decades. Read carefully. You'll likely
find it's 90% impressively right and 10% subtly off. **That ratio is its
character reference.** Assume the same ratio on subjects where you *can't*
spot the 10% — that's exactly where the habits above earn their keep.

## Don't let this put you off

None of this makes AI useless — it makes it *human-shaped* in its flaws. You
already know how to deal with a brilliant friend who occasionally gets details
wrong: you enjoy the brilliance and verify the details. Do the same here, and
you'll get the best of it with very little of the risk. Now for the fun part:
