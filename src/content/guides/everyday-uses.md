Enough theory. Here are ten uses that work today, for ordinary life. Each comes
with an example you can copy into a chatbot word-for-word — or better, adapt to
your own situation. (New here? Start with
[your first conversation](/guides/your-first-conversation/) and come back.)

## 1. Letters and emails you keep postponing

The complaint to the insurance company, the note to the landlord — the ones
that sit on your conscience. Tell the AI the situation in your own words and
let it produce the first draft. You edit; sending a draft nobody wrote but you
is not cheating, it's delegation.

> Help me write a firm but polite email to my phone company. They've raised my
> bill twice this year and I want the old price or I'll switch.

## 2. Cooking with what's actually in the house

> I have eggs, potatoes, half a leek and some cheese. Give me three simple
> dinner ideas, easiest first.

Follow-ups work wonders: "vegetarian", "for someone who shouldn't have much
salt", "I don't own an oven".

## 3. A patient computer helper

The genuinely life-improving one. Instead of feeling stuck or waiting for a
grandchild's visit, describe what you see:

> My printer says "offline" and the documents won't print. Explain what to
> check, one step at a time, and wait for me to tell you what happened before
> the next step.

That last part — *one step at a time, wait for me* — turns the AI into a calm
helpdesk that never sighs.

## 4. Understanding official letters

Letters from the tax office, the bank, the pension fund — written as if to a
lawyer. Type in the confusing part (leave out ID numbers) and ask:

> What is this letter actually telling me, and do I need to do anything?

## 5. Explaining, at exactly your level

> Explain what a "QR code" is to someone who grew up with paper tickets. Then
> tell me why restaurants use them now.

The magic phrase is "explain it like…" — like I'm new to this, like I'm
twelve, like I know nothing about finance. It calibrates instantly and never
condescends.

## 6. Planning trips and days out

> We're two adults visiting Edinburgh for three days in October. We walk
> slowly and love history and good coffee. Suggest a relaxed day-by-day plan.

Then refine: "less walking on day two", "add a rest after lunch". Double-check
opening hours before you go — [AI gets those wrong](/guides/when-ai-is-wrong/).

## 7. Words on the tip of your tongue

> What's the word for when you enjoy someone else's misfortune? And is there an
> English word for it, or only the German one?

Crossword-solvers and letter-writers: this one alone is worth the visit.

## 8. Gift ideas that aren't socks

> A gift under £30 for my brother-in-law who's turning 70, restores old radios
> and claims he already has everything. Ten ideas, please.

Ask for many ideas, keep the two good ones. That's how professionals use AI
for idea work: quantity in, judgement out.

## 9. Reviving what you used to love

Rusty French, abandoned watercolours, the guitar in the attic:

> I learned French forty years ago and remember more than I think. Have a
> simple conversation with me in French, correct my mistakes gently, and stay
> patient.

## 10. A rubber duck for your thoughts

Perhaps the most underrated use: thinking out loud. Weighing a move, drafting
a difficult phone call, sorting out what you actually think. The AI asks decent
questions and never gossips. **It is not a doctor, a lawyer or a friend** — for
the big things, humans first. But for untangling everyday knots, it's a
remarkably useful wall to bounce off.

---

## The pattern behind all ten

Notice what these have in common: **you bring the situation and the judgement;
the AI brings drafts, patience and suggestions.** You stay the editor-in-chief.
That division of labour is the whole art of using AI well — and you now know
it. Everything else on this site is just practice.
