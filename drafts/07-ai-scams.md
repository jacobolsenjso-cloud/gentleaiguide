Here is the honest situation: the scams themselves are old. What's new is that AI has made them **better made**. The phone call sounds like your grandson. The email has no spelling mistakes. The video of the famous investor looks real.

You don't need to understand the technology to protect yourself. You need to recognise five patterns — and keep three habits. That's this whole article.

## 1. The voice on the phone that sounds like family

**What happens:** You get a call. It's your grandson, or your daughter, and something is wrong — a car accident, an arrest, a hospital abroad. They're upset. They need money now, and they beg you not to tell anyone.

**What's new:** With a few seconds of someone's voice — from a social media video, a voicemail, anything public — AI can now copy it well enough to fool a parent. This is called *voice cloning*. It doesn't mean the scammer knows your family. It means they found a recording and a phone number.

**The tell:** Urgency plus secrecy. Real emergencies survive a five-minute pause. Scams don't.

**What to do:** Say "I'll call you right back," hang up, and call the person on the number you already have for them. Not the number that just called you. If they're fine, you've lost nothing. Many families now agree on a **safe word** — a silly, private word that a caller in trouble must say. A cloned voice won't know it.

## 2. "Your computer has a problem" — and someone very helpful wants to fix it

**What happens:** A pop-up on your screen says your computer is infected, with a phone number to call. Or someone rings, claiming to be from Microsoft, Apple or your internet company. They are calm, patient and professional. They ask you to install a small program so they can "have a look."

**What's new:** AI now writes the scripts and the pop-ups, so they sound and look exactly like the real companies. The person on the phone may be reading from an AI-generated script that adapts to what you say.

**The tell:** Nobody at Microsoft or Apple knows your computer has a problem. They don't watch it. They will never call you first.

**What to do:** Close the pop-up — turn the computer off if you have to. Hang up on the caller. Never install anything because a stranger asked. If you're worried, call the company yourself, using the number on your bill or their real website.

## 3. The new friend who is always there

**What happens:** Someone friendly messages you — on Facebook, on a dating site, sometimes just a "wrong number" text that turns into a chat. They are attentive, kind, and interested in you. Weeks go by. Then there is a problem only money can solve: a stuck parcel, a medical bill, an investment "we could do together."

**What's new:** A scammer used to manage a few conversations at a time. With AI writing the messages, they can run hundreds, each one warm and personal, at all hours. You are not talking to a person who cares. You may not be talking to a person at all.

**The tell:** They never meet you, never video call (or the video is short and strange), and money comes up. Always, eventually, money.

**What to do:** Never send money to someone you have not met in person. Not once, not "just this time." Tell a friend or family member about the relationship early — people who are isolated are targeted precisely because no one is there to say "hang on."

## 4. The famous person who wants you to invest

**What happens:** A video appears in your feed. A well-known investor, TV presenter or politician is explaining a new opportunity — and they sound convincing, because it's their face and their voice. There's a link. The returns are remarkable.

**What's new:** This is a *deepfake* — an AI-made video of a real person saying things they never said. They are now cheap to make and hard to spot at a glance.

**The tell:** Famous people do not need your money and do not recruit strangers on social media. And no real investment promises a fixed, high, guaranteed return.

**What to do:** Don't click. If you're curious, search the person's name plus "scam" — these fakes are usually well documented within days. Never invest through a link someone sent you.

## 5. The email that finally has no spelling mistakes

**What happens:** An email from your bank, the tax office, a delivery company or a subscription service. Something needs your attention: a payment failed, a parcel is waiting, your account will be closed. There's a button.

**What's new:** For years, bad spelling and odd phrasing were the giveaway. AI writes perfect English now. The logo is right, the tone is right, the layout is right.

**The tell:** The *link*, not the words. Real companies don't ask you to log in through an email button, and they don't threaten to close your account by tomorrow.

**What to do:** Don't click anything in the email. Open your browser, type the company's address yourself (or use your bookmark), and log in there. If there's really a problem, you'll see it. If there isn't, you've just dodged it.

## The three habits that stop all five

You'll notice the same advice keeps appearing. That's because scams change, but the defence doesn't:

1. **Slow down.** Every scam runs on urgency. "Now, tonight, before it's too late." Real life almost never works that way. Give yourself five minutes. Make a cup of tea. The scam falls apart in the pause.
2. **Go in through your own front door.** Never use the phone number, link or button *they* gave you. Call the number on your card. Type the website yourself. Ring your grandson on the number in your phone.
3. **Tell someone.** Scammers love silence — "don't tell your family," "keep this between us." The moment you say it out loud to another person, it usually becomes obvious.

## If it has already happened

It happens to intelligent, careful people every day — the scams are designed by professionals to work on exactly you. There is no shame in it. Do these things, in this order:

- **Call your bank straight away.** If money moved recently, they can sometimes stop it.
- **Change the password** on any account you shared, starting with your email.
- **Report it.** In the US: reportfraud.ftc.gov. In the UK: actionfraud.police.uk. Elsewhere: your local police non-emergency number. Reports are how these operations get shut down.
- **Tell someone you trust.** Not to be scolded — to have a second pair of eyes on the next call.

## One last thing

Reading this article doesn't make you paranoid. It makes you the person at the table who says, "Hang on — let's just ring them back first." That person saves other people's money, too.
